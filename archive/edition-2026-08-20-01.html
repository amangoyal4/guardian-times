Mechanism of the dayFoundations

How to sell ₹4,451 crore of stock without crashing the price

TPG's sale of a 6.66% stake in Aster DM Quality Care was not executed on your screen. Understanding the mechanics of the 'block deal' reveals the parallel market where institutions operate, a market built on a necessary fiction: that the deal has no price.

The fundamental problem for a large shareholder like TPG is illiquidity. The daily trading volume for most stocks, even large ones, is a fraction of the company's total shares. Attempting to sell a 6.66% stake through the normal market would trigger every automated trading algorithm, creating a cascade of selling that would collapse the price long before the full stake was sold. The very act of selling would destroy the value of the asset being sold. This is the 'price impact' risk, and it makes the open market unusable for institutional-sized exits.

The solution is the block deal: a single, privately negotiated transaction executed on a special trading window of the stock exchange. In India, a block deal must be for a minimum of ₹10 crore. The entire transaction occurs at a fixed, pre-agreed price between the buyer and seller. This price can be no more than 1% above or below the previous day's closing price. The key is that the deal is arranged *before* it ever hits the exchange, insulating it from the chaos of the public order book.

Step 1 — Building the Book. The seller, TPG, hires an investment bank as a broker. The broker’s job is not to place an order, but to discreetly find buyers. Over hours or days, the banker confidentially calls a curated list of potential counterparties: large mutual funds, insurance companies like LIC, sovereign wealth funds, and other private equity firms. They gauge interest and build a 'book' of demand for the shares, effectively pre-selling the entire block.

Step 2 — The Discount. The block is almost always priced at a discount to the prevailing market price. This is not a sign of desperation; it is the explicit, negotiated fee the buyer receives for providing liquidity. The buyer absorbs a massive position instantly, taking on the risk that they may not be able to exit it as easily. The discount compensates them for this service. A typical discount ranges from 2% to 7%, depending on the liquidity of the stock and the size of the block.

Step 3 — The Worked Example. Let's model the TPG-Aster DM deal. The transaction value is ₹4,451 crore for a 6.66% stake. Suppose Aster DM's closing price the day before the deal was ₹520 per share. The broker, acting for TPG, might offer the block to institutional buyers at a 5% discount, or ₹494 per share. A large mutual fund might agree to buy ₹1,000 crore worth, an insurer ₹1,500 crore, and so on, until the full ₹4,451 crore block is spoken for. Once the book is full, the broker executes the matched trade on the exchange's block deal window. The public sees a single, massive trade print on their screens, but by then, it is a fait accompli.

The second-order consequence is signalling. A large shareholder exiting via a block deal can be interpreted negatively, suggesting they believe the stock is fully valued. However, the identity of the buyers is a powerful counter-signal. If the shares are bought by well-respected, long-term domestic institutions (like mutual funds or insurers), the market often interprets this as a positive transfer of ownership from a purely financial owner (the PE fund) to more stable, 'strong hands'.

The third-order consequence affects the stock's future. The removal of a large, motivated seller (an 'overhang') can be a long-term positive. Other investors, who may have avoided the stock fearing that a large PE exit would flood the market, can now invest with more confidence. This can lead to a re-rating of the stock's valuation multiple in the months following the block deal, as the ownership structure is perceived as more stable.

The mechanism breaks down when there is no demand. The broker's phone calls can fail to build a book, especially in a falling market or if the company's prospects are poor. This is precisely what is happening with the ₹39,000 crore of bad loans being offered by public sector banks. Despite the 'discount' to face value, there are no takers. A block deal is not magic; it is a pricing and distribution mechanism that cannot create demand where none exists.

The takeawayA block deal is not a market sale; it is a private placement executed publicly. For investors, the key signals are not the sale itself, but the size of the discount and the quality of the buyers who absorb the shares.
